The record

Every morning of the war, the clerk of Battery C filled out a card. Who was present, who was sick, who had been promoted, and — in four or five handwritten lines under Record of Events — where the battery was and what it had done.

Those cards survive: 284 microfilm frames covering the battalion from the pier at Brooklyn to the end of the war in Europe. They are the spine of this site. Where a claim here has a date attached, it comes from a card, and the card is cited.

The soldier

Box by box, as the Army recorded him on the day it let him go. A thirty-seven-year-old farmer from Tuscola County when he sailed, and thirty-nine when he came home.

The crossing

The battery left Fort Slocum by harbor boat on the evening of 2 May 1944, boarded an Army transport at Terminal Pier at 2315, and cleared the New York Port of Embarkation at 0630 the next morning. The morning report does not name the destination — convoy destinations were not written on a form that travelled with the ship.

One hundred eighteen men were on the roll. One hundred seventeen were present for duty.

The destination is filled in eighteen months later, on his discharge: England, 15 May 1944. Twelve days at sea.

      The theater

      A field artillery battalion moved constantly and rarely far — a few miles forward to a new firing position, then a few miles more. Plotted against the campaign as a whole, those small displacements trace the shape of the advance.

      The battalion, by the numbers

      On 24 August 1945 the battalion adjutant typed out Special Orders 66: one hundred forty-two men, by name and serial number, transferred to the 70th Infantry Division to go home. It is the closest thing in the film to a roster.

      Each dot below is one man, keyed to his Army serial number. The document records only one relationship between any two of them — that they held the same military occupational specialty, and so did the same job — so that is what the lines mean. Men who worked alongside each other cluster together. Colour is the Adjusted Service Rating, the points score that decided who went home first.

      The Bronze Star

      The Bronze Star was established in 1944 for heroic or meritorious achievement in ground combat. The second half of that phrase is the point. It was made for the soldier who did necessary, unglamorous, dangerous work well, for months on end.

      Cole's citation is a good example of what that meant in a firing battery. On a busy day a battery could send hundreds of rounds downrange. Someone had to know, at every hour, exactly how many rounds of each type were on hand, get more forward over roads under fire, and be certain that what arrived was still safe to put down a tube. That was his section.

      Citation — Award of Bronze Star Medal

      Sergeant Clarence J. Cole, 36106875, Field Artillery, Battery C 153d Field Artillery Battalion, United States Army, for meritorious service in support of active combat operations from 30 June 1944 to 15 March 1945 in France, Germany and Belgium. Sergeant Cole supplied the battery with its ammunition supply records, maintained the basic load at all times and also made continuous checks to ascertain that the ammunition was in no way damaged in transit. Showing marked qualities of leadership, Sergeant Cole has managed his section expertly and has performed his duties in an exemplary manner. Entered military service from Michigan.

      Transcribed from the original in the family papers. See the document.

      Still open

      The general orders number and the date of the award are on neither the citation nor the discharge. Awards are normally announced in the morning reports, so both may be in the frames still to be read.

      Sources & method

      How to read a morning report

      The upper table lists changes in status — a man moving from duty to hospital, a promotion, a transfer — each with a serial number, name, grade, and a code. The block below it records the unit's strength that day. The handwritten paragraph in the middle, Record of Events, is the only free text on the form, and it is where the war shows up.

      On uncertainty

      These are carbon copies of pencil, photographed onto microfilm eighty years ago. Where a reading is uncertain it is marked as such rather than smoothed over.
