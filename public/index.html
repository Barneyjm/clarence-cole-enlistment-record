The record

Every morning of the war, the clerk of Battery C filled out a card. Who was present, who was sick, who had been promoted, and — in four or five handwritten lines under Record of Events — where the battery was and what it had done.

Those cards survive as 284 microfilm frames, covering the battalion from New York to the end of the war in Europe. Every dated statement on this site comes from one of them, and names the frame it came from.

The soldier

His discharge record, box by box. He was a thirty-seven-year-old farmer from Tuscola County when he sailed, and thirty-nine when he came home.

The crossing

The battery left Fort Slocum by harbor boat on the evening of 2 May 1944, boarded an Army transport at Terminal Pier at 2315, and cleared the New York Port of Embarkation at 0630 the next morning. The morning report does not name the destination — convoy destinations were not written on a form that travelled with the ship.

One hundred eighteen men were on the roll. One hundred seventeen were present for duty.

The destination is filled in eighteen months later, on his discharge: England, 15 May 1944. Twelve days at sea.

        The theater

        A field artillery battalion moved often and rarely far: a few miles forward to a new firing position, then a few miles more. Plotted together, those moves follow the line of the advance.

        Every position below is a village the battery named in its own station entry, joined in the order it reached them: Omaha Beach to the Cotentin, across France in the pursuit, into Belgium, and over the German frontier. The battery logged 979 road miles for itself in the process.

        The battalion, by the numbers

        Two orders, three weeks apart, replaced most of the battalion. On 24 August 1945 Special Orders 66 transferred 142 men to the 70th Infantry Division for return to the United States. On 11 September the 29th Infantry Division, which was also going home, assigned 241 of its men to the 153rd. The two orders name 383 soldiers.

        The point scores explain the exchange. Almost every man leaving on SO 66 had 78 or more. Almost every man arriving on SO 226 had fewer. Men with enough points went home. Men without them were transferred to units that stayed.

        Each dot is one man, keyed to his Army serial number. These documents record just one relationship between any two of them — that they held the same military occupational specialty, and so did the same job — so that is what the lines mean. Men who worked alongside each other cluster together.

        The Bronze Star

        The Bronze Star was established in 1944 for heroic or meritorious achievement in ground combat. Cole's award was for meritorious service: work done well over a long period, rather than a single act.

        His citation describes ammunition supply. A battery could fire hundreds of rounds in a day. Someone had to know how many rounds of each type were on hand, bring more forward over roads under fire, and check that what arrived was undamaged. That was Cole's section.

        Citation — Award of Bronze Star Medal

        Sergeant Clarence J. Cole, 36106875, Field Artillery, Battery C 153d Field Artillery Battalion, United States Army, for meritorious service in support of active combat operations from 30 June 1944 to 15 March 1945 in France, Germany and Belgium. Sergeant Cole supplied the battery with its ammunition supply records, maintained the basic load at all times and also made continuous checks to ascertain that the ammunition was in no way damaged in transit. Showing marked qualities of leadership, Sergeant Cole has managed his section expertly and has performed his duties in an exemplary manner. Entered military service from Michigan.

        Still open

        The general orders number and the date of the award appear on neither the citation nor the discharge. Awards were normally announced in the morning reports. The frames covering that period have not been read.

        The maps they were working from

        Every morning report ends its record of events by naming the sheet in use — (Map Bonn 1:100,000 Sheet S-1.) — and opens its station line with a position on that sheet: Schmidthof 1 Mi N wF8935 Nord de Guerre Zone. Between them they give a firing position to the kilometre, on a named map, on a dated day.

        Two grid systems appear: Lambert Zone I in Normandy, and the Nord de Guerre zone from the Seine onward. Both are Lambert conformal conics on nineteenth-century French ellipsoids, and both need a datum shift of more than a kilometre to match a modern map. The lettered squares are not taken from a manual. They are worked out from the reports, by requiring each village the battery named to agree with the square written beside it.

          Sources & method

          How to read a morning report

          The upper table lists changes in status — a man moving from duty to hospital, a promotion, a transfer — each with a serial number, name, grade, and a code. The block below it records the unit's strength that day. The handwritten paragraph in the middle, Record of Events, is the only free text on the form, and it is where the war shows up.

          On uncertainty

          These are carbon copies of pencil, photographed onto microfilm eighty years ago. Where a reading is uncertain it is marked as such rather than smoothed over.
